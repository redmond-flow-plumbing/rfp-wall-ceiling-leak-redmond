Where Mystery Stains Actually Come From

In two-story Redmond homes, the upstairs bathroom is suspect number one. A failed toilet wax ring lets water seep with every flush, traveling along the subfloor before staining the ceiling below. Shower pans develop hairline cracks, tub overflows leak at the gasket, and supply lines to sinks develop pinhole leaks that mist inside the wall cavity for months. But not every ceiling stain is plumbing — and misdiagnosing costs money. Roof leaks follow a different pattern: they grow after rain and often track along rafters, while plumbing leaks are steadier and tied to fixture use. Air conditioning condensate lines clog and overflow drain pans, mimicking a pipe leak almost perfectly. Water heater relief valves discharge through walls. Our first job on every call is ruling things out, because opening a ceiling for a roof problem — or calling a roofer for a plumbing problem — wastes everyone's week.

How We Trace a Leak to Its True Source

We work from evidence, not hunches. Moisture meters tell us how wet the materials are and, more importantly, where the wettest point is — water is always wettest nearest its source. Thermal imaging cameras reveal temperature differences: a cold supply-line leak shows as a cool signature inside a warm wall, even through intact drywall. Then comes the process of elimination. We run each fixture above the stain one at a time — toilet flush, shower on, sink running — and watch the meter. Nine times out of ten, one fixture makes the numbers jump. When we do open a wall or ceiling, the opening is small, deliberate, and placed where patching is invisible afterward. We'd rather spend an extra twenty minutes diagnosing than cut three exploratory holes. Precision here is what separates a $400 repair from a $1,400 drywall restoration project.

Drying: The Step Everyone Skips

Here's what separates a proper job from a future mold problem: drying. Wet drywall, insulation, and framing don't just "air out" behind a patched ceiling — trapped moisture grows mold within 24 to 48 hours, and you'll smell it long before you see it. After we expose the leak area, we measure moisture content in every affected material. Saturated insulation gets removed (it never fully dries in place). We set up directed airflow and dehumidification and verify with the meter that framing is back to normal moisture levels before anything gets closed up. Sometimes this adds a day to the timeline, and some homeowners chafe at the wait. But closing wet materials inside a wall is how a $500 leak becomes a $5,000 mold remediation six months later. We won't skip it, and you shouldn't let anyone else skip it either.

Repairing the Pipe — and the Damage

Only after the source is found and everything is dry do we repair, and the order matters: plumbing first, finishes last. The pipe repair itself depends on what failed — a pinhole in copper gets a proper soldered or press-fit repair, a cracked ABS joint gets cut out and replaced, a failed shower valve gets rebuilt or swapped. We pressure-test the repair before closing anything, because the only thing worse than one leak is a second one behind fresh drywall. Then comes restoration: insulation replaced, drywall patched, texture matched to your existing finish, and paint.
